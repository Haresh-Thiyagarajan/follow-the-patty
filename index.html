The outbreak

A timeline of January 1993, plus what the USDA did next. Click an entry to read the note.

  1. Doctors in the Seattle area see children with bloody diarrhea and a kidney problem called hemolytic uremic syndrome (HUS). From January 1 to 29, 230 people in Washington have lab-confirmed E. coli O157:H7, and 80 more are waiting on test results (CDC, Feb. 1993).

    Twist: the CDC later found earlier cases in California, going back to Nov. 15, 1992. So the outbreak was first noticed in Washington, but it may not have started there.

  2. Dr. Phil Tarr, a children's doctor in Seattle, reports a cluster of sick kids to the Washington State Department of Health. Sources disagree on whether this was January 12 or 13.

    The bacteria is E. coli O157:H7.

  3. Investigators compare 16 early cases with people who did not get sick. The sick people had eaten at Jack in the Box. Of those who remembered their meal, 92% ate a regular-size patty (CDC).

    On January 18 the Washington Department of Health announces the source at a press conference. In total, 73 restaurants in four states were involved. [CONFIRM: 73 comes from Wikipedia, find a better source]

  4. The company stops serving hamburgers and recalls its unused patties in several states. Only about 272,672 patties are recovered. That is 20% of the bad ones. The rest had already been eaten (CDC).

    Final CDC count: more than 500 lab-confirmed cases and 4 deaths, all children. Washington had 477 cases, Nevada 58, California 34 and Idaho 14. Other sources count 732 people sick in all.

  5. Dr. H. Russell Cross runs the USDA's Food Safety and Inspection Service (FSIS) during the outbreak. He is one of the authors of the Murano 2018 article, so that article is an insider's view.

    The USDA adds safe handling labels to packages of raw meat. On Sept. 29, 1994, it says raw ground beef with E. coli O157:H7 counts as "adulterated" and cannot be sold. In October 1994 it starts testing ground beef for it.

Note: The CDC counted illnesses from Nov. 15, 1992, to Feb. 28, 1993. Washington cases peaked January 17 to 20.

Cause and effect

Five steps from the outbreak to the 1996 rules. Open each one and decide as a class how much it explains.

  1. What happened

    People got sick from E. coli O157:H7 after eating at Jack in the Box. It was first noticed in Washington, where doctors saw sick children. More than 500 confirmed cases and 4 children died.

    Source

    CDC reports from 1993, and Murano 2018, "The Outbreak That Changed Meat and Poultry Inspection Systems Worldwide."

    Evidence strength: strong

    The CDC investigated it at the time and published the numbers, so the basic facts are solid.

    Class: how much does this explain?

  2. What happened

    Juska and others argue that a concentrated beef industry, where a few big companies handle most of the meat, is part of why contamination outbreaks happen. In 1992 the four biggest beef packers handled about 78% of steer and heifer slaughter (USDA Economic Research Service). So one bad batch could reach a lot of people.

    Source

    Juska 2003, "Manufacturing Bacteriological Contamination Outbreaks in Industrialized Meat Production Systems."

    Evidence strength: medium

    It is a real study, but the authors are sociologists who are critical of the meat industry, so they may lean one way.

    Class: how much does this explain?

  3. What happened

    Juska 2000 splits U.S. meat safety standards into four stages: before 1982, after 1982, after 1993, and after 1996. The 1993 outbreak marks the start of its own stage.

    Source

    Juska 2000, "Negotiating Bacteriological Meat Contamination Standards in the US."

    Evidence strength: strong

    The authors studied the standards directly and give reasons for each stage. They are the same authors as step 2, though.

    Class: how much does this explain?

  4. What happened

    In 1996 the government put new rules in place, called "Pathogen Reduction; Hazard Analysis and Critical Control Point (HACCP) Systems." It was a USDA rule from July 25, 1996, not an act of Congress. Under HACCP, meat companies monitor their own plants instead of inspectors checking every carcass.

    Source

    Dunn 2007, "Escherichia coli, Corporate Discipline and the Failure of the Sewer State." Also Juska 2000.

    Evidence strength: strong

    Two separate sources describe the same change. This is about what the rules did, not why they happened.

    Class: how much does this explain?

  5. What happened

    The outbreak came first and the rules came three years later. That part is clear. Whether the outbreak caused them, or only came before them, is the hard part.

    Source

    Murano 2018 says in its title that the outbreak changed inspection. Juska 2000 describes the standards as negotiated, which suggests other forces mattered too.

    Evidence strength: medium, and debated

    Coming first is not the same as causing. The source that says it clearly is an insider, and the others point to more causes than one outbreak.

    Class: how much does this explain?

Then and now

How ground beef was inspected and how burgers were handled, before and after.

Topic Before 1993 After 1996
Who checks the meat Government inspectors checked every carcass by sight, smell and touch, sometimes called "poke and sniff." Companies monitor their own plants with HACCP plans. Inspectors check that the plans and records are being followed.
Rules in place Inspection based on the 1906 Federal Meat Inspection Act. E. coli O157:H7 had been linked to hamburgers since 1982, but there was no special rule for it. Pathogen Reduction and HACCP rule, July 1996. Big plants had to follow it by 1998, smaller ones by 1999 and 2000.
Testing beef for E. coli No USDA testing of ground beef for E. coli O157:H7. Since 1994 the USDA tests ground beef for it, and beef that has it cannot be sold. Plants also test carcasses for E. coli.
How hot burgers are cooked Federal standard was 140°F. Washington state law already said 155°F. The FDA raised its recommendation to 155°F.
How restaurants handle patties Jack in the Box cooked to the federal 140°F instead of Washington's 155°F. Jack in the Box hired food scientist David Theno, tested its beef for E. coli, checked cooking times, and used HACCP in its restaurants.

What stayed the same: E. coli outbreaks kept happening after the new rules. Juska 2003 asks whether the rules really worked. For example, in 2007 Topps Meat recalled 21.7 million pounds of ground beef after about 30 people in 8 states got sick.

My conclusion

Draft. I will edit this before I present.

The 1993 Jack in the Box outbreak put real pressure on the government to change how ground beef is checked. By 1996 the rules had changed. Companies now had to run HACCP plans and watch their own plants, instead of inspectors checking every carcass.

I think the outbreak was a big cause of the change, but not the only one. The rules were negotiated, and the beef industry had its own problems before 1993. [CONFIRM my final answer]

Sources

  • Juska, Arunas, et al. "Manufacturing Bacteriological Contamination Outbreaks in Industrialized Meat Production Systems: The Case of E. coli O157:H7." Agriculture and Human Values, vol. 20, no. 1, Mar. 2003, pp. 3-19. https://doi.org/10.1023/A:1022416727626.

    Why it is useful: Shows why outbreaks happen in a concentrated beef industry and asks if the new rules worked.

    Limitations: Sociologists critical of industry, covers the whole country, ends in 2003.

  • Dunn, Elizabeth. "Escherichia coli, Corporate Discipline and the Failure of the Sewer State." Space and Polity, vol. 11, no. 1, Apr. 2007, pp. 35-53. https://doi.org/10.1080/13562570701406568.

    Why it is useful: Explains HACCP as companies monitoring themselves instead of inspectors checking every carcass.

    Limitations: A theory argument for academics, little detail on 1993.

  • Juska, Arunas, et al. "Negotiating Bacteriological Meat Contamination Standards in the US: The Case of E. coli O157:H7." Sociologia Ruralis, vol. 40, no. 2, Apr. 2000, pp. 249-71. https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-9523.00146.

    Why it is useful: Four stages of meat safety standards (before 1982, after 1982, after 1993, after 1996) and the reasons for each.

    Limitations: Same authors and point of view as the 2003 article, covers the whole country, ends around 2000.

  • Murano, Elsa A., et al. "The Outbreak That Changed Meat and Poultry Inspection Systems Worldwide." Animal Frontiers, vol. 8, no. 4, 2018, pp. 4-8. https://doi.org/10.1093/af/vfy017.

    Why it is useful: Insider view. One author, H. Russell Cross, ran the USDA food safety agency (FSIS) from 1992 to 1994.

    Limitations: Written 25 years later in an industry-supported journal, and short.
